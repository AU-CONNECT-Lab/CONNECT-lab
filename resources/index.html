What is fNIRS?

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is one of our core research tools. Here's what it is, how it works, and why we use it to study adolescent brain development.

A window into the working brain — without a scanner

fNIRS is a non-invasive brain imaging method that measures brain activity by detecting changes in blood oxygen levels just below the skull. It uses harmless near-infrared light to capture what the brain is doing — in real time — while participants move, talk, and interact naturally.

💡

How it works

fNIRS shines near-infrared light (700–900 nm) through the scalp and skull. Because oxygenated and deoxygenated blood absorb light differently, sensors on the scalp detect hemodynamic changes — a proxy for neural activity — across the cortex.

👦

Why it's ideal for teens

Unlike fMRI, fNIRS is portable, quiet, and tolerant of movement. Participants can talk, make facial expressions, and interact — making it ideal for studying the kinds of real social situations that matter to adolescents.

🧪

What we measure

We focus on prefrontal cortex activity — central to emotion regulation, decision-making, and social cognition — and how it responds to stress, connection, and social context in adolescents across diverse backgrounds.

🌍

In real-world settings

Because fNIRS is wearable, it can be used in homes, schools, and communities — letting us study the brain in the environments that actually shape it, closing the gap between lab measurement and lived experience.

✅

Safe and painless

fNIRS uses only light — no magnetic fields, no radiation, no needles. It is completely safe for children, adolescents, and adults. The headband-style equipment is comfortable and easy to put on in minutes.

📊

What the data looks like

fNIRS produces time-series signals showing increases and decreases in oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin across scalp channels, mapping which brain regions are more or less active during different conditions.
